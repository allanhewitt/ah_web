1. Sampling

Why can two samples tell us different things?

From description to inference

What does it mean to take a sample?

Sampling means selecting a smaller group of athletes to measure so that we can learn about a larger group.

Population = the athletes we want to understand. Sample = the athletes we actually measure. Random sampling = selecting athletes by chance rather than choosing only those we expect to perform well.

A different sample can give a different estimate, even when nothing else changes.

Sampling and random allocation are not the same

Sampling asks who enters the study? Random allocation asks which condition does each participant receive?

They both involve chance, but they solve different problems.

Start with a population

Select riders to study.

Sampling affects which athletes conclusions can apply to.
Then allocate conditions

Assign those riders to placebo or rinse by chance.

Random allocation helps make the treatment groups comparable.

2. Variation and uncertainty

What changes when individual athletes vary?

From description to inference

Standard deviation: how much riders differ

Standard deviation (SD) measures how widely individual riders’ times vary around their group mean.

The rinse group is more spread out: SD 33.7 s versus 19.0 s for placebo. SD describes riders, not confidence in the treatment effect.

The mean tells us the centre. The standard deviation tells us how far individual times spread around it.

Standard error: how much study results vary

Standard error (SE) describes how much the estimated difference between groups would vary if we repeated the study with new riders.

The estimated benefit is 27.0 s. Its SE is 10.44 s: a measure of sampling uncertainty, not variation among individual riders.

How can we make a study more precise?

What usually goes wrong

  • Reporting an SD when you mean the precision of the group difference.
  • Assuming twice the riders halves the SE: it takes roughly four times as many.
  • Treating a 27 s estimate as precise without reporting uncertainty.

An effect estimate without its precision is not enough to judge.

3. Confidence intervals

How certain are we about the estimated effect?

From description to inference

Step 1: begin with the observed difference

Study A gives us one number: the placebo group was 27.0 seconds slower than the rinse group on average.

We want to estimate the average effect for the wider population, not only these 27 riders.

Step 2: allow for uncertainty

The estimated difference has a standard error of 10.44 seconds. An interval allows for the uncertainty in the estimate.

Estimate±margin of error
For a 95% interval in Study A, the margin is about 2.08 × 10.44 = 21.7 seconds.

The 2.08 multiplier comes from a t distribution and accounts for the small sample size.

Step 3: build the 95% interval

Start at 27.0 seconds. Go 21.7 seconds below and above it.

27.0 − 21.7to27.0 + 21.7

Step 4: what can the interval tell a coach?

Compare the whole 95% interval with two reference points: 0 seconds (no benefit) and 20 seconds (the coach’s worthwhile threshold).

The study supports a positive average benefit, but it does not yet establish a benefit of at least 20 seconds.

What does “95% confidence” actually refer to?

The 95% refers to the method for making intervals, not a 95% probability for one particular interval.

Across many repetitions of a study, about 95% of properly constructed intervals would contain the fixed, unknown population effect, when the assumptions hold.

A single interval either contains the true effect or it does not. In a real study, we do not know which.

Explore: some intervals miss the truth

What am I looking at? Each horizontal line is the 95% interval for the rinse benefit from one imagined study. The dashed line is the true benefit, which we know only because we built the simulation.

What should I do? Generate 25 intervals, then another 25.

What should I notice? Most intervals cross the true value, but some do not. The 95% describes the method across repetitions.

Why is the truth shown? Only in this simulation do we know the population value. In a real study it remains unknown.

Why use Welch’s t-test?

The rinse trial compares the mean times of two independent groups. Their variability is different, so Welch’s t-test is a suitable starting analysis.

The test uses the difference between group means, its standard error, and a small-sample adjustment to calculate a confidence interval and a t-statistic.

In R, t.test(time ~ group, data = ...) performs Welch’s two-sample t-test by default. The p-value will be explained in the next part of the session.

What are degrees of freedom?

Degrees of freedom count how many values are still free to vary once something has been estimated from the data.

Two groups, unequal SDs (Welch)

Why it matters: an SD calculated from few values is itself uncertain. Degrees of freedom tell the test how much information that SD rests on.

What is the bootstrap? Draw new samples, with replacement, from the 27 riders observed; calculate a gap each time, then take the middle 95% of those gaps.

Interpret the 95% correctly

  • It does not mean a 95% probability that the true value is inside this particular interval.
  • It means the procedure covers the true value in about 95% of repeated studies, when its assumptions hold.
  • Always check the direction of subtraction: here placebo minus rinse.

This study does not identify one precise benefit; it gives a range compatible with the data.

Practical reading: The interval describes uncertainty in the average benefit, not a range of how much every individual rider will improve.

4. Hypothesis testing

How surprising is the result under no effect?

From description to inference

Begin by imagining no effect

The null hypothesis is a starting assumption used to judge whether the observed gap is unusual.

For Study A, Welch’s test starts from zero average benefit. A simple label-shuffle illustration starts from the stronger assumption that no rider benefits.

Neither assumption means we have proved the rinse does nothing. The test asks what patterns we would see if the chosen null were true.

A p-value measures surprise under that starting assumption

A p-value asks: if the null hypothesis were true, how often would a test produce a statistic this extreme or more extreme?

A small p-value suggests the observed result is unusual under the null model. It does not give the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

Assume

Set the null model.

Compare

Consider results it would generate.

Count

How often are results at least this extreme?

Picture the p-value

If the rinse did nothing, gaps between the groups would still vary by chance. The p-value is the share of that pattern at least as far out as what we saw.

A small p-value means the observed gap sits far out in the tails of the no-effect pattern.

Why both tails? Before the study we did not know whether the rinse would help or harm, so unusually large gaps in either direction count. This is a two-sided test.

Explore: shuffle the group labels

What am I looking at? The bars show gaps after reassigning placebo and rinse labels among the 27 observed times. Orange lines mark ±27.0 s.

What should I do? Shuffle the labels and count gaps at least as extreme as the observed one.

What should I notice? Large gaps are unusual under the sharp null. This does not say the probability the null is true.

What does ±27 s mean? We count unusually large gaps in either direction: at least 27 s faster or 27 s slower.

The shuffle test in R

set.seed(1700)
shuffled <- replicate(10000, {
  labels <- sample(groups$condition)
  mean(groups$tt_time_s[labels == "placebo"]) -
    mean(groups$tt_time_s[labels == "rinse"])
})
observed_gap <- summary_A$mean[1] - summary_A$mean[2]
mean(abs(shuffled) >= abs(observed_gap))
about 0.0165Shuffle test
0.017Welch test

The tests are close here, but they involve different null hypotheses and assumptions. The shuffle reallocates the 27 observed times between groups of 13 and 14 riders. Under the no-effect assumption, differences arise from random allocation.

Read the last line: abs() ignores the sign and mean(...) gives the fraction of shuffled gaps at least as large as the observed 27 s (about 1.65%).

Is p = 0.017 enough?

The rinse trial gives p = 0.017 and an estimated benefit of 27 seconds. Would you recommend changing every rider’s pre-race routine?

What additional information would you want before making that decision?

A p-value does not answer everything

  • p = 0.017 is not a 1.7% chance that there is no effect.
  • A p-value is not the size of an effect.
  • A p-value alone does not establish whether the intervention is worthwhile.
  • A non-significant finding is not proof of no effect.

The result would be unusual under the null. We still have to ask whether the size matters.

Put 0.017 into words: If the null model were true, a test result this extreme or more extreme would occur in about 1.7% of comparable studies.

5. Evidence and decisions

Does an effect need to be worthwhile as well as detectable?

From description to inference

Does the result justify changing practice?

The coach specified a smallest worthwhile change (SWC) of 20 seconds before seeing the data.

Study A provides evidence of an average benefit. The 95% confidence interval crosses 20 seconds, so the evidence is insufficient to conclude that the average benefit exceeds 20 seconds.

Why 20 s? The coach chose this minimum benefit before the trial because smaller savings were not worth changing the race-day routine.

Five ways an interval can sit

Ask two questions of any interval: does it exclude 0, and is it entirely above the worthwhile line?

The faint line at −20 s: a slowing of 20 s would matter too. An interval sitting wholly between −20 and +20 s supports “too small to matter either way”.

What should I notice? A positive estimate is not enough: whether the whole interval lies above 20 s changes the conclusion.

What is half-width? It is the distance from the central estimate to either end of the interval. A wider interval means more uncertainty.

Evidence for a benefit is not the same as evidence that it exceeds 20 s.

Read the R options: mu = 20 asks whether the average advantage exceeds 20 s; alternative = "greater" means we only test benefits larger than that. The p-value of 0.25 does not establish that it exceeds 20 s.

A threshold is a decision, not a discovery

  • The worthwhile threshold must be specified and justified before inspecting the result.
  • A confidence interval crossing 20 s leaves the decision uncertain.
  • Equivalence (“too small to matter”) requires a separate test and defensible bounds.
  • The reading’s magnitude-based inference debate illustrates why verbal probabilities can mislead.

What matters is the estimate, its uncertainty, and the cost of acting or not acting.

Equivalence: A separate analysis asks whether the effect is small enough to be considered practically unimportant. A non-significant ordinary test cannot establish that.

Why does pairing change the answer?

A cyclist is usually fast or slow in both test conditions. What information do we lose if we pretend their two times came from two unrelated riders?

Which would you trust more: comparing different riders, or comparing each rider with their own second time? What new problem could the second design introduce?

A one-sample test asks whether a squad average differs from a known reference value, rather than comparing two groups.

Design comes before the test

  • Choose independent, paired or one-sample methods from the study design.
  • Counterbalancing helps against common order effects but does not rule out carryover.
  • Before-and-after change can reflect regression to the mean.
  • Never pair unrelated rows just because they occupy the same positions.

Study B gives the coach stronger evidence, but only if the crossover design was valid.

Regression to the mean: People selected for unusually poor first results can improve on a retest even without any intervention.

ANOVA compares the spread between groups with the spread within groups.

New vocabulary: A negative split means riding the second half faster. ANOVA stands for analysis of variance: it asks whether the three group averages differ more than expected from ordinary variation within the groups.

What does an F-statistic compare?

ANOVA compares variation between group averages with variation within the groups.

If the group averages are far apart relative to the ordinary spread of individual riders, the F-statistic becomes larger.

Between groups

How separated are the three strategy averages?

Within groups

How much do riders vary within each strategy?

F = 3.69 means the adjusted between-group variation is 3.69 times the within-group variation. It does not say which pair differs.

What am I looking at? Each circle is a rider. The short horizontal marks are group means. The live F ratio compares between-group to within-group variation.

What should I do? Increase separation, then increase the within-group spread.

What should I notice? F tends to rise as groups move apart, and fall when riders within groups become more variable.

What is F? The F-statistic is a ratio: variation between the group averages divided by variation within the groups, after accounting for the number of groups and riders. A larger F suggests the group means are more separated relative to individual variation.

What if the method changes the conclusion?

The conventional ANOVA gives p = 0.036; an alternative ANOVA that allows unequal group variation gives p = 0.068.

Would you report only the first result, only the second, or both? What would be your message to the track coach?

Why do the p-values differ? Conventional ANOVA treats the groups as having equal variability. Welch ANOVA allows their variability to differ, giving p = 0.068. Neither result should be selected just because it is more favourable.

ANOVA with two groups is the same as Student’s t-test: F equals t². ANOVA simply extends the t-test to more groups.

Rank-based (non-parametric) tests replace each value with its position in order. They protect against outliers and skew, but they answer a different question from a test of means.

What a rank-based test sees

Ranks keep the order and discard the distances. One extreme rider can no longer pull the result around.

The price: the test is no longer about the average number of seconds saved, which is what the coach asked about. Choose a rank-based test because its question fits, not to rescue a result.

One unusually slow rider can strongly change an average without changing much about the ranks.

Reading these: the methods broadly agree here, but each answers a slightly different question. A Kruskal–Wallis result says the distributions differ somewhere; it does not say which strategy is faster, and differences in spread or shape can drive it. Decide which question you are asking before running any of them.

What usually goes wrong

  • Running several unadjusted t-tests and reporting only the smallest p-value.
  • Assuming a significant omnibus ANOVA says which groups differ.
  • Ignoring differences in group variability.
  • Treating the three strategies as causal without checking how riders were allocated.

A coach needs the actual difference between strategies, not just the F statistic.

Omnibus means “overall”: The ANOVA F-test asks whether at least one group mean differs; follow-up comparisons identify where the difference may lie.

8. Effect sizes and small studies

How big is an effect, and when might it be exaggerated?

From description to inference

Start with the effect in seconds

The raw effect is the difference in the original units: Study A estimated that the rinse saved 27.0 seconds on average.

27.0 sObserved mean difference
20 sCoach’s smallest worthwhile change

In this case, seconds of difference make the result meaningful to a coach. Even so, we need to compare the estimate with its interval before deciding.

Cohen’s d puts an effect on a standard-deviation scale

A standardised effect divides a difference by a measure of variation, allowing comparisons across outcomes that use different units.

27.0 sRaw difference
27.6 sPooled SD used for d
d = 0.98Difference in SD units

Cohen’s d describes the size relative to variation. It does not tell the coach whether the benefit is worthwhile.

A caution: The pooled SD used here assumes a shared underlying SD, whereas the primary Welch analysis allowed the two group SDs to differ. Always report which d was calculated.

What should I notice?The raw difference stays at 27 seconds. The standardised difference becomes smaller as variability increases.

What does “low-powered” mean? When a true effect exists, a low-powered study has a small chance of detecting it by a significance test. The few that do often have unusually large estimates.

Paired effects need a clearly named version of d

In Study B, the same 39.1-second average saving can be divided by two different measures of variation.

diffs <- cross$placebo_s - cross$rinse_s
d_z <- mean(diffs) / sd(diffs)
d_av <- mean(diffs) /
  mean(c(sd(cross$placebo_s), sd(cross$rinse_s)))
c(d_z = d_z, d_av = d_av)
2.45dz: using variation in individual savings
0.82dav: using variation in riders’ times

The benefit has not changed. Only the denominator used to standardise it has changed.

Why it matters: For paired data, reporting “d = 2.45” without the subscript can mislead readers into comparing it directly with an independent-groups d.

Simulate selection of significant results

set.seed(1700)
studies <- t(replicate(10000, {
  p <- rnorm(14, 1530, 30)

Type M: average magnitude exaggeration among selected significant results. Type S: estimates with the wrong sign.

Power and exaggeration: Power = 13% means only about 13 out of 100 studies detect the real 10 s effect. Among the studies that do, estimates are on average about 2.8 times the true magnitude. This is a Type M error.

Can a significant study exaggerate a benefit?

Suppose a small trial reports a 28-second improvement with p below 0.05, but the true average improvement is only 10 seconds.

Why are the significant results from small studies especially likely to look impressive? How could coaches protect themselves from overreacting?

Why replication matters

  • A small study can produce a large estimate by chance.
  • Selecting only “significant” studies creates an exaggerated published picture.
  • Report raw effect units, intervals, sample size and design before standardised labels.
  • Type M and Type S remind us that significance does not guarantee the estimate is accurate.

The 27 s estimate from Study A may be an overestimate. The paired Study B adds evidence; further replication would strengthen it.

Type S: The rarer error in these examples is getting the direction wrong, so a genuinely helpful rinse appears harmful or vice versa.

9. Interpreting evidence responsibly

What should we report beyond a threshold and a p-value?

From description to inference

Should p = 0.05 be a dividing line?

There are good arguments against mechanical thresholds, and arguments for carefully justified statistical tests.

Critique of thresholds

Results just above and below 0.05 are almost the same evidence. A single cut-off encourages exaggerated claims.

Case for tests

When chosen in advance and interpreted properly, hypothesis tests can support decisions with explicit error rates.

The shared position: Statistical tests can be useful, but the result should also report its size, its uncertainty, the study design and why any threshold was chosen.

Explore: a cliff at p = 0.05?

What am I looking at? The scale shows a continuous p-value, with a line at 0.05 often used as a decision threshold.

What should I do? Compare 0.049 and 0.051. Is there a sharp change in the strength of evidence?

What should I notice? The evidence changes gradually; the label changes suddenly.

Compare the numbers: 0.049 and 0.051 provide nearly the same strength of evidence. A binary “significant/not significant” label makes them seem very different.

Report the analysis, not only the test

report <- tibble(
  effect_s = diff(rev(welch_A$estimate)),
  lower_s  = welch_A$conf.int[1],
  upper_s  = welch_A$conf.int[2],
  p_value  = welch_A$p.value,
  worthwhile_s = 20
)
report |> mutate(
  excludes_zero = lower_s > 0,
  supports_worthwhile = lower_s > worthwhile_s
)

Include design, estimate, interval, threshold, method, limitations and the plain-language decision.

Why build it from welch_A? Taking the numbers from the test object, rather than typing them, means the report cannot drift from the analysis. What will R show? excludes_zero = TRUE and supports_worthwhile = FALSE: the interval excludes no benefit but includes benefits below the coach’s 20 s target.

Choose methods before seeing the answer

  • One-sample question → one-sample t-test.
  • Two independent groups → Welch’s t-test.
  • Same riders twice → paired t-test.
  • Three independent groups → ANOVA plus justified comparisons.
  • Worthwhile change → interval relative to a pre-specified threshold.

No single p-value can replace a sound design and a clear question.

The rule: Start with what was measured and on whom. Only then select the test that answers the question.

A complete result answers five things

  • Design: Who was studied, and how?
  • Estimate: What happened, in meaningful units?
  • Uncertainty: How precise is the estimate?
  • Method: Why does this test fit the design?
  • Decision: What does it mean against the practical threshold?
