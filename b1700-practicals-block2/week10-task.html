Modelling Relationships

Student Task Sheet · Riverside Cycling Club

Why this matters

This session assumes the pre-class reading has been completed. We move from comparing groups (Week 9) to describing and predicting from relationships between variables, then to predicting a yes/no outcome.

Central question: is an observed relationship strong enough to describe, and to predict from?

PRE-READING CHECKPOINT

In one sentence each, explain:

  • what a correlation coefficient tells you, and one thing it does not;
  • what the slope and the intercept of a regression line each represent;
  • what regression to the mean is;
  • why an ordinary straight line is the wrong tool for a 0/1 outcome.

CONSEQUENCE PROBLEM

Riverside's coaches want to know whether weekly training volume predicts 10-mile time-trial performance, and whether average power output predicts a top-10 season finish. Before touching R, identify: which of these two questions produces a number (a predicted time), and which produces a probability (a predicted chance of an event)? Why does that distinction determine which type of model is appropriate?

PART ONE — TRAINING VOLUME AND TIME-TRIAL PERFORMANCE

Work in week10_session.R.

The file week10_riders_training.csv contains, for 24 riders, average weekly training hours over the previous eight weeks and their time in a 10-mile time trial. Lower times are better.

Task 1 — Plot the relationship

Plot time_trial_min against weekly_hours. Describe the direction and strength of the relationship you see.

Task 2 — Correlation

Calculate the Pearson correlation coefficient with cor(). What does its sign and magnitude tell you? What does it not tell you?

Task 3 — Fit the model

Fit model <- lm(time_trial_min ~ weekly_hours, data = riders) and inspect summary(model). Identify the intercept, the slope, and the R².

Task 4 — Interpret the coefficients

Write one sentence interpreting the slope in minutes per additional hour of weekly training. Write one sentence explaining why the intercept should not be read as a realistic prediction here.

Task 5 — Confirm R² = r²

Confirm that R² equals r² for this model. Explain, in your own words, why this equality is specific to a single-predictor regression.

Task 6 — Regression to the mean

Riverside's coaches are excited that a rider who trained unusually little this month recorded an unusually fast time. Using the idea of regression to the mean, explain why that rider's next result is unlikely to be quite as extreme, even with no change in ability.

Task 7 — Confounding

Suggest one plausible confounding variable for the training-volume/time-trial relationship, and explain how it could produce the observed correlation without training volume having a direct causal effect.

Checkpoint: you should be able to state the fitted equation, explain what each coefficient means in context, and explain one reason the relationship might not be causal. If you cannot do all three, revisit the reading before continuing.

PART ONE EXTENSION — ONE-HOT CODING WITH THREE RECOVERY PROTOCOLS

Open week10_recovery_protocol.csv. It contains recovery time (minutes) for 60 athletes randomly allocated to one of three post-session recovery protocols: standard, active, contrast.

Task 8 — Set up the factor

Set protocol as a factor with standard as the reference level. Create a boxplot comparing recovery time across the three protocols.

Task 9 — Fit and read the dummy regression

Fit lm(recovery_min ~ protocol, data = recovery) and inspect summary(). State what the intercept represents, and what each of the two dummy coefficients represents relative to the reference group.

Task 10 — Compare with the ANOVA

Which protocol(s), if any, differ significantly from standard? Compare the model's omnibus F-statistic with what a one-way ANOVA on the same data would report.

Task 11 — Dummy coefficient vs Tukey comparison

In one sentence, explain why a significant dummy coefficient here is not the same claim as a significant Tukey comparison from Week 9's ANOVA follow-up.

PART TWO — PREDICTING A TOP-10 FINISH

This part has less scaffolding than Part One. Build a complete, defensible analysis rather than following numbered sub-steps.

Open week10_riders_top10.csv. It contains average power output (watts) in a qualifying time trial for 60 riders, and whether each rider went on to finish in the top 10 of the season-ending race (made_top10, already coded 0/1).

Your analysis should produce:

  • a logistic regression model predicting made_top10 from avg_power, fitted with glm(..., family = binomial);
  • the odds ratio for a one-watt increase, and a rescaled odds ratio for a 10-watt increase, with a one-sentence interpretation of each;
  • predicted probabilities for every rider, and a classification at the 0.5 threshold;
  • a confusion matrix, with accuracy, sensitivity and specificity calculated from it;
  • the baseline accuracy from always predicting the majority class, and a judgement of whether the fitted model clears that baseline convincingly;
  • one sentence on what sensitivity and specificity each tell a coach that accuracy alone does not.

Task 12 — Recommend or not

Would you recommend this model to Riverside's selectors as it stands? Justify your answer using the sensitivity and specificity you calculated, not accuracy alone.

Task 13 — Moving the threshold

Suggest one reason the threshold might reasonably be moved away from 0.5 for this specific use case (selecting riders to invest development time in), and explain which error type your suggested change would trade off against the other.
