PRE-READING CHECKPOINT

In one sentence each, distinguish:

  • standard deviation from standard error;
  • an observed mean difference from a population mean difference;
  • a confidence interval from a p-value;
  • Type I error from Type II error.

CONSEQUENCE PROBLEM

A priming warm-up group appears faster than a standard warm-up group. The performance staff had already decided that an improvement of 0.08 seconds would justify changing practice.

Before touching R, identify the four quantities you need in order to move from an observed sample difference to a defensible decision. Then write, from memory if you can, the two relationships introduced in the reading:

SE = √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂) and t = observed difference / SE.

You do not need perfect notation. The aim is to show that you understand how the quantities connect before software produces them.

PART ONE — WARM-UP STRATEGY AND 20 M SPRINT PERFORMANCE

Work in week09_session.R.

The file week09_core_warmup.csv contains one post-warm-up sprint time for each athlete. Athletes were randomly allocated to either a standard warm-up or a priming warm-up, and each athlete appears once.

Smallest worthwhile difference: 0.08 seconds. Lower sprint times are better. Define all differences as standard minus priming.

Task 1 — Establish the design

Confirm the grain, grouping variable and outcome. Explain why this is an independent two-group comparison, why random allocation strengthens the causal interpretation, and why each athlete appearing only once matters for that independence.

Task 2 — Describe before testing

Calculate n, mean and SD for each group. Create a boxplot and write one sentence about direction, spread and overlap, and a second sentence on whether any individual point looks unusually extreme relative to the rest of its group.

Task 3 — Calculate the standard error

Calculate the raw mean difference in seconds, then evaluate the standard error SE = √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂). State what each quantity represents.

Task 4 — Predict, then test

Predict t ≈ observed difference / SE before running the test. Then run Welch's t-test using t.test() and compare R's t statistic, df, p-value and 95% CI with your prediction.

Task 5 — Interpret the interval

Explain what the 95% CI says relative to 0.00 s (no population difference) and 0.08 s (the pre-specified worthwhile threshold).

Task 6 — Interpret the p-value

Explain the p-value correctly, then state what it does not tell you about effect size or practical importance.

Task 7 — Recommend

Write a three-sentence recommendation to the performance staff incorporating magnitude, uncertainty, statistical evidence and practical importance.

Checkpoint: your inferential chain should now be explicit — means + SDs + n → SE → t and df → CI and p → practical decision. If those quantities feel disconnected, go back one step rather than jumping straight to the p-value. Do not continue until you can explain each link.

NOW ON YOUR OWN — SWIMMING FEEDBACK AND 50 M PERFORMANCE

Open week09_transfer_swimming.csv. Each swimmer appears once and received either standard technical feedback or video feedback. The file does not establish how swimmers were allocated.

Lower times are better. Define the difference as standard feedback minus video feedback. The smallest worthwhile improvement is 0.30 s.

Repeat the sequence above on this dataset, this time without individual step-by-step prompts. Your result should include: the descriptive comparison and plot; the raw mean difference and SE; t, df, p and the 95% CI; interpretation relative to 0.00 s and 0.30 s; a practitioner conclusion; and one sentence on why the statistical comparison is possible but a causal claim is not automatic, given the unknown allocation mechanism.

PART TWO — THREE FURTHER SITUATIONS

Part One worked through one technique in full. Part Two extends the same logic — design, describe, predict, test, interpret, decide — to three further situations you will meet regularly in practice, each with its own dataset.

A. One-sample t-test: is this squad unusual?

Open week09_challenge_onesample_cmj.csv: countermovement jump (CMJ) height for 16 netball players. A published sub-elite benchmark is μ₀ = 28 cm.

  • State the null and alternative hypotheses for comparing this squad's mean CMJ against the fixed benchmark.
  • Calculate n, mean and SD. Create a plot and write one sentence about shape and any unusual values.
  • Calculate the standard error SE = s/√n, then predict t ≈ (x̄ − μ₀) / SE before running the test.
  • Run t.test(cmj$cmj_height_cm, mu = 28). Compare R's t, df, p-value and 95% CI with your prediction.
  • Explain what the 95% CI says about plausible values for the squad's true mean CMJ, relative to the 28 cm benchmark.
  • Write one sentence a strength and conditioning coach could act on.

B. Paired t-test: did coaching change reaction time?

Open week09_challenge_paired_reaction.csv: reaction time (ms) for 14 sprinters, recorded before and after four weeks of block-start coaching. Lower is better.

  • Explain why this is a paired rather than an independent-groups design. What would be lost by analysing it as two independent groups?
  • Calculate each athlete's within-athlete difference (before minus after), then the mean and SD of those differences.
  • Calculate the standard error SE = s_d/√n, then predict t ≈ d̄ / SE before running the test.
  • Run t.test(reaction$reaction_before_ms, reaction$reaction_after_ms, paired = TRUE). Compare R's output with your prediction.
  • Explain what the 95% CI says about the plausible size of the true change, and whether zero remains plausible.
  • Write one sentence a sprints coach could act on, and one sentence on what the paired design does and does not rule out as an explanation for the change.

C. One-way ANOVA: which warm-up protocol works best?

Open week09_challenge_anova_plyo.csv: 45 athletes randomly allocated to one of three plyometric warm-up protocols (control, depth_jump, hurdle_hop), with CMJ height recorded afterwards.

  • Explain why comparing all three protocols with three separate t-tests would be a weaker approach than a single ANOVA.
  • Calculate n, mean and SD for each protocol. Create a boxplot comparing all three groups.
  • Set protocol as a factor with levels control, depth_jump, hurdle_hop, then run aov(cmj_height_cm ~ protocol, data = plyo) and summary(). Report F, df and p for the omnibus test.
  • If the omnibus test is significant, run TukeyHSD(). Which protocol(s) differ from control? Do the two treatment protocols differ from each other?
  • Write one sentence a coach could act on, and one sentence explaining what the omnibus F-test does not tell you on its own.

D. Further reflection

  • Precision without changing the effect. Imagine the Part One warm-up study produced the same group means and SDs but with twice as many athletes in each group. Explain what would happen to the SE, CI width, absolute t statistic and p-value. Which quantity would remain unchanged?
  • Different practical thresholds. One coach would act on a 0.05 s improvement; another requires 0.12 s. Using the same estimate and CI from Part One, explain why they can reach different practical decisions without disagreeing about the statistics.
  • Standardised effect size. Calculate Cohen's d = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / s_pooled for the Part One warm-up comparison. Using the conventional benchmarks (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large), how would you describe this effect? What does d add to the raw seconds figure, and what does it lose?
